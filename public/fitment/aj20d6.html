If your Land Rover is badged D300 or D350, it could be either the 306DT V6 or the newer AJ20D6 straight-six — the badge alone doesn't tell you, and the two are completely different engines under the skin.

  • The 306DT is a 3.0 V6 diesel, fitted roughly 2010–2021 depending on model
  • The AJ20D6 is a newer 3.0 straight-six diesel, introduced from around 2020–2021
  • Both have been sold under the D300 and D350 badges, at different times, on different models
  • The most reliable identification method is the engine number stamp, not the badge [PATTERN DATA]
  • Ordering the wrong engine due to a badge mix-up is the costliest version of this mistake — always confirm before committing to a quote

Why Two Engines Share One Badge

JLR names badges after power output, not engine architecture — which is exactly why this confusion exists.

"D300" describes a diesel producing roughly 300PS. When the outgoing 306DT V6 was replaced by the newer AJ20D6 straight-six, both engines were tuned to produce approximately the same power figure, so both qualified for the same badge. From a marketing standpoint this makes sense — the badge tells a buyer what the car will feel like to drive. From an engine-identification standpoint, it means the badge alone is genuinely useless for working out which physical engine you have [PATTERN DATA]. This is not unique to the D300 badge — the same pattern occurs on the petrol side with P400, and JLR has used this naming convention consistently across the range for over a decade, which is worth knowing if you're researching a different badge and assuming the number tells you the engine family.

Why This Matters When Buying a Replacement

The two engines are not interchangeable in any sense that matters to a buyer. Mounting points, ECU calibration, ancillary compatibility and even the physical dimensions of the block differ enough that a supplier quoting for "a D300 engine" without confirming which architecture you actually have is quoting blind. Ordering the wrong one means a return, a delivery delay, and in most cases a second shipping cost on top of the original — entirely avoidable by confirming the exact engine code before requesting a quote, not after.

Common Misidentifications

The specific wrong answers people arrive at, and why.

  • Assuming badge year = engine year. The changeover happened mid-cycle on most models, not at a clean model-year boundary.
  • Ordering a 306DT replacement for a post-2021 D300. This is the costliest version of this mistake — the two engines are not interchangeable, and a wrong-engine order means a return, a delay, and a second shipping cost.
  • Confusing AJ20D6 with AJ126. AJ126 is a petrol V6; AJ20D6 is a diesel straight-six. The "AJ" prefix is shared corporate naming, not a family relationship.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is AJ20D6 the same as 306DT?

No. They are two genuinely different engine families that happen to share some badge names. The 306DT is a 3.0 V6 diesel used from roughly 2010-2021. The AJ20D6 is a newer 3.0 straight-six diesel that began replacing it from around 2020-2021, badged D300/D350 in some models.

How do I know if my D300 has a 306DT or an AJ20D6?

Check your build date. Most D300-badged models switched to the AJ20D6 from the 2021 model year onward, though the exact changeover point varies by model. The most reliable method is checking the engine number stamped on the block, or using our VIN decoder tool.

Is the AJ20D6 reliable?

It is a newer engine, so our enquiry dataset is still building. Early indications from our 2025 data are positive, with no catastrophic failure pattern yet comparable to the 306DT's crankshaft bearing issue. We will update this page as more real-world mileage accumulates.

Why does JLR use the same badge for two different engines?

JLR badges describe approximate power output rather than engine architecture. Both the outgoing 306DT and the newer AJ20D6 were tuned to produce roughly 300PS, so both qualified for the D300 name even though they are mechanically unrelated.
