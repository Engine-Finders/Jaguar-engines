AJ6/AJ16 Oil Capacity, Grade & Service Data

Reference figures for Jaguar's classic straight-six engines, fitted across XJ, XJS, and other models. No opinions — just the numbers, correctly sourced and verified against workshop manuals.

The AJ6 and AJ16 engines take approximately 6.5 litres with filter change [PLACEHOLDER — confirm vs OEM workshop data], to a 10W‑40 or 5W‑40 semi‑synthetic oil meeting API SG/CD or later, with a change interval of 6,000 miles or 12 months [PLACEHOLDER — confirm vs OEM workshop data] — though many specialists recommend more frequent changes for older, high‑mileage units.

  • Capacity varies slightly between AJ6 and AJ16, and by sump design — see the variant table below
  • Real‑world caveat: sludge buildup and hydraulic tappet noise are common if the oil grade or change interval is neglected [EM-INTERVIEW]
  • Applies across XJ40, X300, XJS and XJ-SC with the 3.6/4.0 litre engines, as well as the later AJ16 in X300 and XJS

Notes & Caveats

The manufacturer's 6,000‑mile interval assumes regular use and standard driving conditions. In practice, many specialist workshops recommend shortening the interval to 4,000–5,000 miles for cars that are used infrequently, have high mileage, or show signs of sludge buildup [EM-INTERVIEW]. The AJ6 and AJ16 are robust, but their hydraulic tappets are sensitive to oil degradation — neglected changes can lead to persistent tappet noise and, eventually, camshaft wear. This is covered in more depth on our timing chain diagnosis guide, though note that the AJ6/AJ16 use a timing chain, not a belt, which is less prone to sudden failure but still requires proper oil pressure to keep tensioners healthy.

Also worth noting: the AJ16 engine (introduced in 1994) benefits from a revised oil pump and better internal baffling, but the oil capacity and specification remained essentially unchanged. The key difference is that later AJ16 cars may specify a higher‑grade oil (e.g., API SJ/CF) to protect the catalytic converters — always check your owner's manual for the exact recommendation.

Common Mistakes

  • Using a modern 0W‑20 or 5W‑30 oil. These are too thin for the older engine design and can lead to low oil pressure, tappet noise, and premature wear — stick to 10W‑40 or 5W‑40 as specified.
  • Ignoring the filter type. Some aftermarket filters have incorrect bypass valve settings, which can bypass unfiltered oil. Always use a quality filter that matches the OEM specification.
  • Overfilling. Adding more than the specified 6.5 litres can cause oil aeration and increased crankcase pressure, leading to oil leaks and reduced engine performance.
  • Confusing the AJ6/AJ16 with the earlier XK engine. The XK (used in Series 3 XJ and earlier) has a different capacity and filter — if you're unsure, check your engine code using our Engine Code Guide.
  • Neglecting the oil cooler lines. On cars fitted with an oil cooler, the capacity may increase slightly; always fill gradually and check the dipstick.

How to Verify on the Actual Vehicle

The dipstick on both AJ6 and AJ16 engines is located on the driver's side (right‑hand drive cars) and is easily accessible from the top. The filler cap is on the cam cover, marked with the oil grade. Before assuming the figures above apply, confirm your engine number — the engine code is stamped on the block near the starter motor or on a metal tag on the cam cover. Use our Engine Number Location guide to decode it. If your car has been modified with a different sump (e.g., aftermarket performance sump), the capacity may differ — in that case, the safest approach is to fill gradually and check the dipstick repeatedly.

Why This Specification Matters

Getting the oil right on an AJ6 or AJ16 is not just about lubrication — it's about maintaining hydraulic tappet function, timing chain tensioner pressure, and overall engine longevity. These engines were designed in an era when oil formulations were different; using a modern low‑SAPS oil that's too thin can cause the tappets to rattle on startup and, over time, lead to camshaft lobe wear. Conversely, using an oil that's too thick (e.g., 20W‑50) can reduce cold‑start flow and increase wear during the critical first few seconds. The specified 10W‑40 or 5W‑40 semi‑synthetic strikes the right balance for UK climates, providing adequate cold‑start protection while maintaining viscosity at operating temperature. For cars that are used on track or in hot climates, some specialists recommend a full‑synthetic 5W‑40, but this should be done only with a corresponding increase in change frequency. The underlying principle is simple: these engines reward regular, correct‑spec oil changes with long, trouble‑free lives — and they punish neglect with expensive top‑end rebuilds.
