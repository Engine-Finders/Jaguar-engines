AJ‑V8 vs AJ133

Two generations of Jaguar V8, head to head. The older AJ‑V8 is a proven, simpler unit with lower running costs; the AJ133 brings modern supercharged performance but carries higher ownership risks. Here’s which one to pick.

The AJ133 wins for performance enthusiasts, but the AJ‑V8 is the smarter buy for most owners — it’s cheaper to replace, easier to work on, and its known failures (chain guides) are less catastrophic than the AJ133’s pre‑2013 timing chain tensioner issues. The AJ133’s extra power and refinement are undeniable, but the cost of ownership is significantly higher.

  • AJ‑V8: £3,000–£5,500 reconditioned S&F (estimate), fewer enquiries [PATTERN DATA]
  • AJ133: 330 enquiries in 2025, £5,500–£9,000 reconditioned S&F [EM‑VERIFIED / EM‑QUOTE]
  • AJ133 pre‑2013 chain tensioner failure is a known catastrophic risk; AJ‑V8 chain guide wear is less severe and more readily repairable
  • AJ133 offers 510–550 PS versus AJ‑V8’s 300–420 PS — a genuine performance gap
  • Over five years, the AJ‑V8 typically costs £2,000–£4,000 less to run, driven by lower parts and labour costs

The Verdict

The AJ‑V8 is the better choice for most buyers — it’s cheaper to replace, has a less catastrophic failure mode, and is simpler to maintain. The AJ133 offers more power and modern refinement, but its pre‑2013 timing chain tensioner risk and higher replacement cost make it a more expensive ownership proposition. If you value performance above all, the AJ133 is the clear winner; for everyone else, the AJ‑V8 delivers a better balance of cost and capability.

AJ‑V8 Deep Dive

The AJ‑V8 family (4.0 and 4.2 litres) powered the XK8, XKR, XJ, S‑Type and early XF. It’s a naturally aspirated or supercharged unit known for its smooth power delivery and robust bottom end. Its main weakness is timing chain guide wear, which typically manifests as a rattle on startup and, if ignored, can lead to chain failure and valve damage [PATTERN DATA] — see our AJ‑V8 Chain Guide Wear page. The repair is well‑understood and, at £3,000–£5,500 for a reconditioned unit, is notably cheaper than the AJ133’s replacement. The AJ‑V8 also enjoys better parts availability because it was produced for over a decade, and many specialists have deep experience with it. Full specs and reliability rating are on the AJ‑V8 Knowledge Centre.

AJ133 Deep Dive

The AJ133 is Jaguar’s 5.0‑litre supercharged V8, found in the XFR, XKR, F‑Type R, and XJ Supersport. It produces up to 550 PS in SVR guise and delivers breathtaking performance. However, it carries a well‑documented pre‑2013 timing chain tensioner failure risk — a catastrophic issue that can destroy the engine [EM‑INTERVIEW]. Post‑2013 units are much more robust, but earlier cars are a gamble without a documented tensioner replacement. Replacement costs are high: £5,500–£9,000 for a reconditioned unit, plus 14–18 hours of labour [EM‑QUOTE]. The AJ133 also suffers from coolant crossover pipe leaks and supercharger coupler wear, both of which are expensive to address. Full details on our AJ133 Chain Tensioner Failure and AJ133 Knowledge Centre.

Where the AJ‑V8 Genuinely Wins

The AJ‑V8’s strengths are its simplicity and lower cost of ownership. It doesn’t have the AJ133’s complex supercharger, direct injection, or high‑pressure fuel system — fewer things to go wrong. Its chain guide wear is a relatively straightforward fix compared to the AJ133’s tensioner failure, which often requires a full engine strip. The AJ‑V8 is also less thirsty: real‑world fuel economy is about 10–15% better than the supercharged 5.0. For owners who don’t need the extra power, the AJ‑V8 delivers a more than adequate 300–420 PS, which is still plenty for spirited driving. In our data, the AJ‑V8 generates fewer enquiries, but that reflects its age and lower market profile, not a higher failure rate. Many specialists recommend the AJ‑V8 as the smarter daily‑driver V8.

Five‑Year Ownership Cost Comparison

Over a typical 5‑year ownership window, the AJ‑V8 is £2,000–£4,000 cheaper to run than the AJ133. This gap is driven by four factors: (1) lower replacement cost when things go wrong, (2) cheaper routine servicing (fewer ancillaries, simpler design), (3) better fuel economy, and (4) lower insurance groups (the AJ133 is typically in a higher performance bracket). The AJ133’s higher performance does command a premium, but unless you regularly exploit that power, the extra cost is hard to justify. For high‑mileage drivers, the AJ‑V8’s lower parts and labour costs make it especially attractive. Our engine replacement cost guide provides a full breakdown of costs for both engines.

Which Should You Buy? — Three Scenarios

🚙

Daily Driver / Budget‑Minded

Choose the AJ‑V8. It’s cheaper to buy, cheaper to run, and its known issues are well‑understood and affordable to fix. Perfect for an XJ or XK used regularly.

⚡

Performance Enthusiast

If you crave the thrill of 550 PS and a supercharger whine, the AJ133 is unmatched. Just budget for a post‑2013 car or factor in a tensioner replacement as preventative maintenance.

🔧

Long‑Term Keeper / DIY Mechanic

The AJ‑V8 is easier to work on and has a vast knowledge base. Parts are cheaper and more available, making it the smarter choice for those who plan to keep the car for years.

Whichever you choose, the same repair vs replace framework applies when the engine needs attention. Always factor in the vehicle’s current value and the cost of a replacement engine.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the AJ133 more reliable than the AJ‑V8?

Post‑2013 AJ133 units are reliable, but pre‑2013 cars have a well‑documented timing chain tensioner failure risk. The AJ‑V8’s chain guide wear is less catastrophic and more affordable to repair, making it arguably more reliable in practice for older cars.

Which engine is cheaper to replace?

The AJ‑V8 is significantly cheaper: £3,000–£5,500 reconditioned fitted versus £5,500–£9,000 for the AJ133. Labour hours are also lower on the AJ‑V8.

Is the AJ‑V8 worth buying over the AJ133?

If you don’t need the extra power, yes. The AJ‑V8 is more economical, cheaper to maintain, and still provides a satisfying V8 experience. The AJ133 is only better if you value performance above all.

What is the 60% scrap threshold for Jaguar?

Jaguar uses a 60% scrap threshold: if the cost of a replacement engine exceeds 60% of the vehicle’s market value, it’s usually cheaper to scrap the car. We apply this rule in our repair‑or‑replace framework.
