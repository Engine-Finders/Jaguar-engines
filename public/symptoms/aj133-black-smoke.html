AJ133 Black Smoke From Exhaust

Black smoke from an AJ133 is a different conversation to the same symptom on any other Jaguar engine. Here's exactly what it means on the 5.0 V8, the components most likely to be responsible, and real cost data if it turns out to be the fault this engine is known for.

On the AJ133 specifically, black smoke from the exhaust is rarely a simple sensor fault. It's almost always a sign that the engine is running rich under load — caused by failing injectors, a leaking high-pressure fuel pump, or in some cases, a failing MAF sensor that can't meter air correctly. A blocked DPF on a diesel this is not — the AJ133 is a naturally aspirated or supercharged petrol V8, and black smoke means unburnt fuel, not soot.

What This Engine Specifically Does

Where our general black smoke guide covers five possible causes across the whole Jaguar range, on the AJ133 the picture narrows considerably. This 5.0 V8's dominant fuel-system failure signature is a cloud of black smoke under hard acceleration — not a constant haze, not a blue-ish tint, but a dense, dark plume that clears once you lift off. It is distinct from the lighter, grey-ish smoke of a failing supercharger coupler (which is more about worn bearings than fuel), and distinct from the white smoke of a coolant leak into the combustion chamber. All three can sound similar to an untrained eye over a phone video, which is why in-person diagnosis matters.

Why This Engine In Particular

The AJ133's direct-injection fuel system runs at extremely high pressure — up to 200 bar on the naturally aspirated version and higher still on the supercharged R and SVR variants. That pressure is precisely what makes it so efficient when it's working, and precisely what causes such dramatic black smoke when it isn't. A leaking injector doesn't just drip; it dumps fuel into the cylinder under full pressure, and the unburnt surplus exits through the exhaust as that characteristic dark cloud. The high-pressure fuel pump (HPFP) itself can also develop internal leaks, sending extra fuel straight into the rail without the ECU knowing. This is a design characteristic of this particular fuel-delivery system rather than a universal V8 trait, which is why the same symptom means something quite different on, say, a previous-generation AJ-V8 with port injection.

How to Confirm It's This, Not Something Else

Before assuming the worst, it's worth ruling out the cheaper alternatives specific to this engine. A genuinely dirty air filter can produce a similar rich-running condition on hard acceleration and costs under £50 to replace yourself — check the filter housing and listen again on the next full-throttle run. A failing MAF sensor can also cause over-fuelling, though on the AJ133 this usually triggers a code alongside the smoke. If the smoke persists after ruling out the air filter and MAF, and particularly if it's accompanied by a noticeable drop in fuel economy or a hesitation under load, that's the point to book a proper diagnostic fuel-pressure test rather than continuing to monitor it yourself.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is black smoke from an AJ133 always a fuel system fault?

It's the most likely cause given how commonly this specific engine presents this way, but not the only one. A dirty air filter, failing MAF sensor, or even a restricted exhaust can produce a similar rich condition, though the pattern of black smoke under hard acceleration is fairly distinctive once you know what to look for.

At what mileage does an AJ133 typically start smoking black?

Most cases in our data fall between 60,000 and 120,000 miles, though this is a risk band rather than a certainty — many AJ133s pass through this range without ever developing the fault, particularly with strong service history and regular long runs.

Can I drive an AJ133 with black smoke from the exhaust?

It's not recommended to continue driving if the smoke is persistent or accompanied by hesitation, misfire, or a drop in fuel economy. A leaking injector can cause serious damage to the cylinder, piston, and catalytic converter. If the smoke only appears briefly under hard acceleration and clears immediately, you may have a short window to get it diagnosed safely — but treat it as an urgent item.

What does it cost to diagnose black smoke on an AJ133?

A proper diagnostic including fuel-pressure testing, injector balance testing, and a smoke test of the intake system typically costs £180–£350 at an independent specialist. This is money well spent: it often identifies the specific faulty component and prevents a misdiagnosed repair that would have cost far more.
