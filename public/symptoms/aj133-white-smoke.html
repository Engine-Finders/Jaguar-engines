AJ133 White Smoke From Exhaust

White smoke on the 5.0‑litre V8 supercharged engine is rarely condensation - it's almost always a sign that coolant is entering the combustion chamber. On this engine, the usual culprit is a failed coolant crossover pipe (a known weakness) or, more seriously, a head gasket failure. What This Engine Specifically Does

Where our general white smoke guide covers a wide range of causes across the Jaguar range, on the AJ133 the picture narrows significantly. This 5.0 V8 supercharged produces white smoke for one of two dominant reasons: coolant entering the combustion chamber via a cracked coolant crossover pipe (the plastic pipe that sits in the supercharger valley) or a head gasket failure allowing coolant to seep into the cylinders. The tell‑tale difference is the smell: coolant smoke has a sweet, syrupy odour, while condensation (which is normal on cold starts) has no smell and quickly clears. On the AJ133, persistent white smoke that doesn't disappear within a minute of driving is a serious warning sign.

Why This Engine In Particular

The AJ133's cooling system has two well‑documented weaknesses. The coolant crossover pipe - a plastic pipe that sits in the valley between the supercharger and the engine block - becomes brittle with age and heat cycling, frequently cracking and leaking coolant. When it leaks externally, you see puddles; when it cracks internally, coolant can be drawn into the intake manifold through a failed gasket or a porous casting, resulting in white smoke. The head gasket is less commonly an issue on this engine than on some others, but it does occur, particularly on engines that have been overheated or run low on coolant. What makes this engine‑specific is the access difficulty: the supercharger sits atop the V8, and the crossover pipe is buried under it, meaning even a relatively inexpensive part can require significant labour to replace.

How to Confirm It's This, Not Something Else

Before assuming head gasket or crossover pipe, rule out the simpler possibilities. Condensation on cold start is perfectly normal and will clear within 30–60 seconds - if the smoke disappears quickly and there's no coolant loss, it's nothing to worry about. Over‑rich fuelling (e.g., from a faulty injector) can produce white smoke, but this is usually accompanied by a strong petrol smell and a check engine light. If the smoke is thick, persistent, smells sweet, and you're topping up the coolant regularly, you're almost certainly dealing with a coolant ingress issue. A coolant pressure test and a block test (hydrocarbon sniff test) are the definitive diagnostic steps - a specialist can usually confirm the cause within an hour.

Verdict

Do not ignore white smoke on an AJ133 - book a specialist this week, not next month. While condensation is normal, persistent white smoke that smells sweet and is accompanied by coolant loss is a clear sign of a coolant leak into the combustion chamber. Driving with a leaking crossover pipe or a compromised head gasket can lead to overheating, cylinder damage, and a far more expensive repair. Frequently Asked Questions

Is white smoke on an AJ133 always a head gasket?

No - the most common cause is a cracked coolant crossover pipe, which is a known weakness on this V8. Head gasket failure is less common but does occur, especially if the engine has been overheated. The key is to diagnose with a block test to confirm the presence of combustion gases in the coolant.

At what mileage does an AJ133 typically start producing white smoke?

Coolant crossover pipe issues often appear between 60,000 and 90,000 miles. Head gasket issues are more common in the 80,000–120,000 mile range, particularly on cars that have not had regular coolant changes.

Can I drive with white smoke from the exhaust?

If it's a small amount of condensation that clears quickly on a cold start, yes - that's normal. If the smoke is persistent, thick, and smells sweet, do not drive the car. Continuing to drive can cause overheating, engine damage, and a significantly larger repair bill.

How do I tell the difference between condensation and a coolant leak?

Condensation disappears within 30–60 seconds of starting a cold engine and has no distinct smell. Coolant smoke is thick, lingers, smells sweet, and is often accompanied by a drop in coolant level over time. A simple test is to hold a clean piece of paper near the exhaust - if it gets wet with a slightly oily liquid, it's coolant; if it's just water, it's condensation.
